Conversation between We all peel the onions participants, Paul Danvers (art therapist) and Amy Plant in the art room at the Day Hospital.

Extract 4

Margaret: But people are marginalised like us and I think they're more marginalised because of the whole thing.

M: Yes, we've been made anonymous and we've been reduced.

Margaret: Which is what happens with people with mental health problems. It's exacerbated the problem.

M: And people probably do think that you do things like nursery school in places like this, well I mean people do tend to make assumptions about people with mental health problems which is that they are somehow backward.

K: That project is not at all a fair representation of the day-to-day work, it's completely different from the work that we do, isn't it?

M: As the only representation.

K: Yes, that's the only exhibition we've had since I've been here, a year and it's not like what goes on everyday.

Amy: And what about the magazine, Victorya, how did you feel about that?

M: I thought that was better than the exhibition.

Amy: I mean, I personally felt that it was a really good insight into...People obviously trusted Grant quite a lot, to be so lucid about their experiences, that's what I thought anyway. Reading that I found it fascinating. Not in a voyeuristic way, but I found it very honest and I got the sense that Grant had a very good presence here, simply because...

M: The interviews do show that people felt they trusted him, I think you're right about that.

K: I found the interviews good. I liked the interviews. Because you felt like you could cover your illness and all that happened. You went into a separate room and talked about the history of yourself.

Margaret: What they should have done, was make this huge, inbetween all the frippery stuff, they should have reproduced all those interviews with really big letters inbetween to show that these were grown-up people who did this, with ideas, imaginations, personalities. A human being, one that you should respect, with a lot of talent.

K: I think that people found it difficult because it's meant to be therapeutic so obviously if people weren't enjoying a task set it was continued with. It wasn't changed or altered or stopped or anything like that. So people felt that they had to go along with something that they weren't terribly into and if at the end they (the artists and organisers) were disappointed with the attitude of people here... From what I've heard... it just sounded like they were angry or upset that they'd taken trouble to do this in the art room and they hadn't had the response they wanted and they shouldn't have been expecting any specific response.

M: They didn't listen to what people were saying around them. People were saying, 'I'm not sure if I feel like doing masks' or whatever.

Paul: It was an experiment wasn't it?

People did feel experimented on...the people who are here were finding it very difficult to get in the door, let alone to paint something or be told to paint something and what with. I mean some people were suffering. We wouldn't be here otherwise, it's not sort of like, you know...it's not an art school.

K: That's what I mean, if it's an art school, that's fair enough. But it's not an art school.

Paul: It's not an art school, but there's a lot of creative energy here.

M: Yes, but it poses the question, are you allowed to show your suffering or are you only allowed to present to the public a happy face?

Paul: There was one guy who went to the show and said something about...and he said he really enjoyed the show and from his point of view the other show presented psychosis, the Marlene Dumas.
