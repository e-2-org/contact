Conversation between Grant Watson and Amy Plant

Grant: We were talking last time about this idea of me interviewing you and how it was important to have your views amoungst all those voices.

Amy: Yes, that's an issue that's run all through, whilst I've been doing this project really...how much of me do I put in...and I think it was Anna Best actually, who said to me 'what you're doing, getting other people's responses, is you.' Which was quite interesting.

Grant: It's your material.

Amy: It's my material, but I think she also meant that she saw that as a way of working...

Grant: A working practice.

Amy: Yes, in a similar way to other projects I've done which have been about going out and making a platform for other people's expression. So creating that space is a practice.

Grant: I guess there's a kind of broader thing about it being your practice, but then a slightly narrower issue is in terms of your opinions and whether your voice in particular comes across and your opinion of the project actually is stated somewhere.

Amy: Yes, I think that is important, because otherwise, it's kind of unfair, to leave my side of it out. But I think as well, my opinions have been very informed by the conversations I've had with people. I suppose when you do that research that's what you're doing, you're trying to see what affect it's had, or how it's worked for other people. So I think now there's no way that my opinions can't be informed by that. And so, I think if I hadn't done that my opinions might be quite different, in terms of the specific projects. So what are my opinions?

Grant: Well, I was thinking whether you were looking at it as a whole programme and if you have a general opinion, and also the specific projects, some that you maybe feel positive about and others that you feel less positive about.

Amy: Well, I think because I've been trying to draw the threads of the different projects together to see what does link them...different things...I suppose one of the concerns has been about different roles and I feel after talking to someone else it was interesting because sometimes that person was coming from a viewpoint of art as outreaching, or art in public working as an outreaching strategy for the gallery, and if it would help to encourage more people to come into the gallery and see the exhibitions there, and I suppose I haven't been looking at in that way at all.

Grant: It's a very practical way of looking at it...

Amy: Yes, how does it function for the gallery and also for the funders, all those agendas that come into play? But for me I was interested in it from an angle of 'what can it tell us about this area, what can it tell us about the people who live here and what's going on here?' And in a way I thought that would be a much more useful approach for the Centre in terms of...

Grant: A research tool.

Amy: Yes, exactly. In terms of a resource, maybe for artists in the future who are working with Camden, but outside, if they are going to continue with public art projects. And then for me it was about looking at what sort of roles can artists play when they go outside. Because for the artist, that idea of attracting a new audience for a gallery space, that's not their agenda, do you know what I mean?

Grant: But it's very important for the gallery to be making noticeable efforts in that direction, isn't it?

Amy: Yes it is, it's important for the gallery, it's important for their funders and so on. But in a way they took risks because, like you said before, that's one of the quotes I've put in, 'it's not actually about making people happy in the public'. It has made a presence but things haven't always worked, it hasn't always been a smooth process.

Grant: There has to be the space for failure within that.

Amy: Exactly.

Grant: Because I think sometimes there's probably a culture of everything has to be successful and when you do your evaluation at the end, you want lots of smiling faces who think it's great, but that's not the reality of working with the public, if people come along and say they didn't enjoy it, that's a kind of valid response, it doesn't invalidate that project.

Amy: Not at all.

Grant: Like the problems with the Hohenbüchler project, it still maintains credibility on a lot of levels despite that.

Amy: Absolutely. I think the main thing is to learn from these things, and to learn from the audiences really, because they are your clients in a way and you're going out there to offer something to them and I think it does get confused with what's going on in the art world as well and keeping a presence with that for the Centre. The Centre obviously feels that with the projects they do outside, you know, they've worked with fairly big name artists, who have international profiles...

Grant: Because for example, some of the Hohenbüchler group would be really happy with...well some of them said they were really happy working in their pottery groups outside of the Day Hospital and that what was they wanted in terms of an art process, but then that wouldn't have any relevance for the Centre.

Amy: No, not in terms of its art world position, not at all. So I've think you've got to be careful about what's appropriate with a different group and how important is that art world status when you're dealing with... I mean that's something I'm questioning I think, but you know, one of the participants I spoke to... I said, 'do you feel, because of the way that things went, do you feel that these two worlds shouldn't meet?' And she said 'no, I think that they should, just as long as it's done sympathetically.' So in a way, it doesn't have to be a problem, it just has to be done very carefully. You can't suddenly transpose things onto an audience like that or into a space, any of the spaces that have been investigated, just taking the formulas that are used in the close confines of the art world and how that functions. It's difficult, you almost have to forget certain formulas and ways of doing things.

Grant: Value systems.

Amy: Yes, but on the other hand, with 'Journeys' I think that worked really well at combining a group of people who aren't professional artists with artists and coming up with something that was quite sleek as well.

Grant: And very interesting to go and see and really nicely displayed in the library.

Amy: Yes it was. And there hasn't been any controversy over that one so in that sense it hasn't been so much to sink one's teeth into, do you know what I mean, it's just been very smooth....

Grant: I think one of the other things is timing, there were so many artists doing so many things in such a short space of time and perhaps those communities need more careful long-term...rather that these experimental, short term, quick things.

Amy: Definately. I totally agree. I think they would have been better off having say four projects, I think seven was too many.

Grant: Even though the Adam Chodzko one was fairly simple to do.

Amy: Yes I suppose so and that one didn't involve any participation.

Grant: It was just in public space.

Amy: That's right...I think that one worked in the sense that it dealt with the surroundings so people could relate to it because it was about where they are or where they could be...

Grant: In a way maybe it was less challenging in terms of the feedback, I don't know, you're the one who knows, it would be less rich in a way, not like the Hampstead Heath project.

Amy: Yes you get a sense of how that space is used and how people feel about that. It's very clear. Whereas a car park is a totally different situation anyway. They don't claim it, they don't say 'this is my space'. It's quite a safe place really a car park, and people appreciated something...

Grant: ...brightening up a fairly boring car park.

Amy: Absolutely.

Grant: And another highly controversial space is the therapy room really.

Amy: Yes I think that the ponds and the therapy room are more private spaces that public spaces, perhaps.

Grant: Because the ponds are about privacy...it's about getting away from the city isn't it?

Amy: Yes, and also a common ground, the therapy room is a place where people share private thoughts.

Grant: And another thing about it is that it is a confidential space and the project was going completely against that and making it public.

Amy: Yes that's right.

Grant: Which is O.K. because they knew that at the beginning.

Amy: Yes, but I think that those people wanted to use it as an opportunity to have a platform and to educate people about mental illness in general. But I don't think it was really the Hohenbuchler's agenda to explore that because for them it was more about the joy of making.

Grant: They weren't particularly talkative about their agenda but what they did say was that they would like to make these private spaces public and in a way I think the participants had a slightly more political agenda which was to talk about their concerns, about their issues and maybe those things didn't match up.

Amy: Perhaps that had a lot to with time as well. Because maybe if they had had more time in the beginning and had got to know them... maybe there should have been more development in the beginning and they could voice those things straight away.

Grant: One of the problems was that the participants, the group actually changed quite a lot and the make-up of the group changed and the original discussions were held with other people who had already left by the time the sisters had arrived. Then other people arrived who hadn't been in the discussions, so that was a problem, timing... the different time scales that people work on. The Hohenbuchlers are working on a project for a certain amount of time and then they go, the participants can maybe be there for two years or something.

Amy: That's quite difficult to deal with unless you work with people on a one-to-one basis and I'm imagining that a lot of that was taking place within that space anyway.

Grant: There was. I think the participants would have liked it if the Hohenbüchlers had said right at the beginning of the first session, 'what would you like to do with this opportunity?' And what happened was, they presented them with this idea of making masks and body parts and I think that the participants didn't really understand what that was all about, what was being communicated with this idea of masks and also I think they felt that perhaps it clashed with their voices being clear and direct, and yet they were being asked to make these things which created a complication of what they were trying to say. I think that was a bit problematic.

Amy: Definitely, yeah. I mean one of them said that it would have been brilliant to have the material that you generated through the magazine, more visible in the exhibition and if there had been... I think as well there was that problem of them not being able to be involved in the installation of it. One of them was upset because some of her work wasn't included and she had made that work specifically about an issue that she had, and she felt that it hadn't been included because it wasn't 'carnival' enough. That it didn't fit in to that mood and aesthetic. Because some of the visitors to the exhibition were quite shocked... about the idea that the work had been made within a therapy context and I think there was a bit of confusion because some of them thought that it was therapy, and the patients and Paul Danvers, the art therapist, were quite adamant that it wasn't therapy, 'this isn't what we normally do'.

Grant: That was all very, very unclear. One of the interesting things is though, I think that artists are often happy to make ambiguous objects and place them in spaces to be read by the public and I think that's something that the participants weren't so comfortable with, the way their work was delivered was in a very ambiguous way and not being artists they probably weren't very comfortable with that so...perhaps they wanted something more direct and a bit more easy to read.

Amy: When I was talking to the art therapist at the day hospital and I was talking about the specific elements of the installation, and saying 'well I can see conceptually how maybe that worked', like the hut in the exhibition for instance, what that stood for. And he said, 'well I don't think that the participants really...they didn't really get into that side of it', and in a way, they shouldn't be expected to. Throughout the whole project the idea has come up about education or art education and how... making things for people who don't have an art education background, because it's something that we in the art world tend to forget, we've all got some knowledge of a context to put it in.

Grant: Some of those people had been art trained in that group.

Amy: They had? Right.

Grant: But you're right, the context wasn't so familiar to them.

Amy: But also within the whole project, across the board. Interpretation. How much interpretation do you give people, for instance. So with the Felix Gonzalez-Torres piece on Lymington Road, there was no signage, no leafleting and people didn't know it was an artwork unless somebody told them, but that generated a whole discussion down that street and there were rumours going round...'when is the street party going to happen?' and in a way for me that's really part of the work, for me that's sort of...

Grant: Going back to that ambiguity.

Amy: The ambiguity and what affect it has on people, what it does make you think of, and the fact it made them think of the possibility of a street party, and then talking to people and finding out what it's like to live down that street a little bit, what kind of community atmosphere is there and finding out things like, 'well actually we tried to have a community barbecue here once but some of the residents stopped it.' And the fact that there is animosity between the two sides of the street. That project worked as a kind of catalyst in a way and it didn't involve any participation from the beginning apart from, I don't know, a meeting with the Tenants Association, or what ever to say 'is it O.K. if we string these light bulbs up?' I think in the beginning I thought that project was the least interesting for me because it didn't...

Grant: It was an existing piece of work...

Amy: Exactly and it was plonked, you know, it didn't involve any consultation, any development with anyone or anything and it actually worked amazingly well...

Grant:...because of it's simplicity somehow.

Amy: Very simple.

Grant: It's even much simpler than the Chodzko piece, it's the most simple piece in a way.

Amy: Yes, you can't go wrong, and the artist isn't even alive so you can't.... and that was a real learning thing for me in a way. I'm not saying that I think this whole process of consultation and participation is a bad idea, but I think if you do go down that road, you've got to be really careful. Because you make promises and sometimes you can't fulfil them and people's expectations get more and more and more. The more you give, the more people want.

Grant: And with the Hohenbüchler project, not only were you going directly into their therapy space, but you were also involving them in an exhibition which was going to happen in an art gallery. So their expectations must have been sky high in a way.

Amy: Yes, that's right. And I don't think it's a bad thing to do, I think it's a great thing...

Grant: It's a kind of audacious thing to do in a way.

Amy: Isn't it? But I think you've got to be really careful, you've got to be really sensitive and really listen....

Grant: And maybe give it slightly more time.

Amy: Definitely. Peter Cross (curator) was talking before about a lot of practical things to do with that project, not enough time to work out that involvement in the installation and it being Christmas in between, all those things were factors.

Grant: Exactly, I mean that project would have been ideal if they would have had three weeks in the therapy room and then three weeks in the gallery, making the show together, I mean that would have been a perfect solution.

Amy: I think the way that 'Journeys' worked, the participants spent a lot of time in workshops at the Centre. That was a kind of reversal. The workshops took place within the Centre and the exhibition took place in a public space. That seemed to work very well.

Grant: I must say when the participants came to the Centre the seemed like they weren't really at home there.

Amy: Yes, I got that impression from them.

Grant: They came for a few visits and then they came to the opening, you know, they weren't there actually as part of what was going on, they were definately there as visitors.

Amy: That definately had an affect on their perception of Camden Arts Centre as well.

Grant: It would have been really nice if they could have been in and out of that place, using it comfortably and then feeling really at home with the installation and then that would have been a really successful completion of what that project set out to do.

Amy: Definitaly. So the Centre has learnt alot from that one.

Grant: But it's an enormously kind of brave project to put on in a way, for everybody.

Amy: Oh definately! Yes, it was. So again, it's great to take these risks, and if everything goes smoothly and successfully, it's a bit boring really isn't it! It can bring out a lot of other issues as well. And I think that's an interesting thing about the projects...what other issues have come out of them that are aside from the art issues, the kind of curatorial issues that we're all interested in. And I think I told you before about putting newspaper articles in the web site.

Grant: Yes I think it's really nice to have that, to extend the range, yes.

Amy: And I've decided against newspapers now, hopefully, it's going to be a few links to web site pages from the BBC News Online, if that works, because I think the BBC is a bit more neutral that newspapers...

Grant: Perhaps!

Amy: In a way! Television in general is a bit more neutral... well, this is another interesting thing, I couldn't just put articles from the Guardian in, that would be my newspaper and it's not for everybody, so... I suppose what I'm trying to do is to look at what the projects have sparked off in people's minds and then you do find out a little bit about what is going on, what people's real concerns are and I think in a way, for me, that's a starting point if you're doing participatory projects, you've got to work with things that those people are already concerned with, or already...

Grant: Which is maybe the problem with the Hampstead Heath project, people weren't necessarily interested in her imaginative life.

Amy: I know. Although a lot of people really related to the magazine. They wrote and they said 'you've really summed up how I feel about the Heath.' Which is great. And she did try to get participation, she put an ad in the local paper inviting...

Grant: She was just working with a very feisty group.

Amy: Yes, and the consultation problem with the pond users, that could have been avoided. There are probably loads of things going on, on the Heath, to make something a bit more active, do you know what I mean, not necessarily a campaign or a protest...

Grant: Like an event on the Heath that people could participate in?

Amy: No, a bit more... well one of the things that's interesting, I think, is that all of these projects have been temporary...

Grant: Which is nice, I think that's the good thing about them.

Amy: It is good, but I think that you can move them through and you can use those temporary things as research for then doing something which can be more permanent in the future, and I'm not talking about a sculpture or a statue, something like that, I'm talking about something which does have a use-value, perhaps.

Grant: Like some system on the Heath, yes, O.K.

Amy: Yes, or even a regular magazine about Hampstead Heath, that can then be taken over by people who use Hampstead Heath who maybe want to sustain something themselves.

Grant: So the artist would actually be going there and identifying needs rather than projecting their practice onto those situations.

Amy: Exactly. But that's just my kind of personal... that's what I like.

Grant: Useful art.

Amy: Yes I like my art to have a function.

Grant: That's interesting actually. That's good.

Amy: And that way you are really giving something to people as well and that way people can warm to artists as well...

Grant: You have to offer them something... people do have a sort of sense of barter, don't they?

Amy: And why shouldn't they? Because they also get really suspicious, 'you're an artist, what do you want from me?' It can be really like that. And I think, actually one of the good things about the projects is that it didn't actually... I don't think any of the projects actually used people to create artworks. Maybe mine! Maybe I've really used people, I've taken their comments and made this thing! But yes, I think you've got to be really careful with that too... I'm sounding really moralistic, aren't I! You've got to be really nice to everyone! I mean sometimes things can get too nice. But you don't necessarily have to deal with nice things, there's lots of nasty things going on...

Grant: But there has to be something in it for them.

Amy: Absolutely, because if they're not interested in art...

Grant: Because the artists get something out of it, they're making work and getting to have an audience for their work...

Amy: That's right.

Grant: Yes, and I think there needs to be something in it for those people that are participating. And actually when you watched the 'Journeys' film, you could see that they had got something out of it, those kids had really enjoyed that project.

Amy: Yes it was very evident. And being able to use new video cameras... I mean how exciting, I wish I was able to do that when I was a kid, I would have loved it. And I think that they were also able to put in that personal expression as well and that was really insightful for hearing people who don't know very much about Deaf issues to see that from a very humanistic point of view. The only adult in the group actually, she decided to film her daily, almost domestic life, and that was great... she specifically filmed the television with the subtitles on it, using the minicom, things like that, how the door bell worked in her house, because she felt, 'I want to use this as a way of showing people, this is how a Deaf person lives.' So it was great that they were able to take that in their own direction. If they wanted to do something fantasy... one of them did a little horror film with a dog running through the woods, or if they wanted to talk about their holidays, then they could...

Grant: They could direct it, they were in control, weren't they?

Amy: They were, but with a lot of facilitation as well. To be able to realise it in the best way.

Grant: And what about you in terms of doing this project? Has it really changed throughout the time, or...

Amy: You mean the process that I've been going through? I think it was fairly simple really, in terms of a way of talking to people.

Grant: So you had a sort of plan that you put into practice and it hasn't radically shifted or...

Amy: No it hasn't radically shifted, the methods that I used to engage people in conversations seemed to work. Not all of them did. I don't know if I told you, the shopping bag...

Grant: The shopping bag technique?

Amy: Yes. I offered to carry people's shopping to their cars so that we could talk about Adam Chodzko's sign on the way! And I was really naive in that respect, but it was something that I just wanted to try, you know. So I ended up giving away little cacti and people got into it, something tangible.

Grant: Oh, that's sweet.

Amy: Yes, they were all a bit like that really.

Grant: So you had a table?

Amy: Yes, like a stall.

Grant: So that kind of bartering thing came back into play, yes.

Amy: One thing... I was talking to Paul Collett about this as well. I often thought that I was adding to urban pollution in a way...

Grant: Oh, that's interesting.

Amy: ...by being one of those people that stops you on the street.

Grant: You could say that about the whole project, it could run the risk of...

Amy: It could run the risk, yes exactly and in a way it's about highlighting those things. There's one whole section on the web site... one of the themes is about living in the city, it's very broad but it's come up so much, I think it would do, it's only natural. But I don't think you can go too wrong if you're asking people for their opinions.

Grant: No because everyone loves to give that. And the Hohenbuchler project was rich with opinions as well as the Hampstead Heath project, that's what you got back.

Amy: Yes lots of very strong opinions, which is what we like, so that's great. And I think in terms of offering, I feel as though I've offered a platform... it's not a huge platform, but it's a little platform for some of those opinions to actually become published and hopefully it will generate more discussions and the discussions will keep on going... forever!

Grant: Scary!

Amy: Yes, but I'll be out of it, I won't need to hear about it anymore!
